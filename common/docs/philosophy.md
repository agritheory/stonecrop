# Stonecrop Design Philosophy

Stonecrop is a framework and component library intended for business software. This document explains the design principles that guide Stonecrop's structure and visual approach. These principles are not prescriptive rules; they are guidelines that explain the _why_ and _when to_ behind design decisions. Implementation details like colors, spacing, specific typefaces and component APIs will evolve with contributions as the community and user base grows. These principles should guide that evolution, ensuring that changes harmonize with the underlying philosophy even as surface elements change.

This document is written for designers and developers building with Stonecrop, stakeholders evaluating its approach, and maintainers making decisions about how the system should grow. If you're looking for implementation guidance, that exists elsewhere in the documentation; this document explains what Stonecrop is trying to do and why.

## 1. F-Shape Reading and Visual Hierarchy

In 2006, Nielsen Norman Group conducted eye-tracking research across 232 users observing thousands of web pages. The resulting study documented a consistent pattern in how users scan information when reading from left to right. Users first read horizontally across the upper portion of content, then move down slightly and read horizontally again, typically covering a shorter distance than the initial scan. Finally, users scan vertically down the left side of the page. Taken together, these three movements create a shape resembling the letter "F". This pattern has been revalidated in subsequent research and remains remarkably consistent across contexts.

The F-pattern is not a flaw or inefficiency in how users read. Rather, it describes the natural scanning behavior for anyone dealing with information-dense interfaces. In business applications, this scanning mode is the primary reading strategy. Users engaging in data entry will use a find-and-complete workflows need to locate specific fields. Users auditing data scan to identify anomalies or verify values. In a densely populated table, the user is scanning the top and left of the page to orient their understanding of the cell(s) they are focused on. In all three cases, the F-pattern reflects an efficient, natural behavior that should be supported, not prevented. 

The practical implication is straightforward: critical information should be placed where users' eyes naturally travel. This means placing the most important data, key metrics, and primary actions in the triangle formed by the top-left corner, top-right corner, and bottom-left corner of the interface. Secondary or tertiary information can occupy the opposing triangle expanding from the bottom-right area without suffering from reduced discoverability.

Certain types of content demand an alternative approach. Charts and data visualizations, require that users enter what researchers call the commitment pattern: careful, line-by-line reading and comparison of values. The chart's internal structure supersedes any consideration of F-pattern layout and generally should achieve a meaningful first impression while allowing discovery of the details within. When users bring established mental models from real-world experience, such as the way postal addresses are traditionally formatted or how phone numbers are written, the designer should honor that familiar structure even if it diverges from F-pattern alignment. Users have years of trained muscle memory and deconstructing information into semantic components may be technically correct but can create friction. In domain-specific contexts where established standards exist, respecting the user's trained eye and existing workflows matters more than imposing F-pattern consistency.


## 2. Tables as Information Default

Microsoft Excel functions as the lingua franca of business problem-solving. This dominance is not accidental; it stems from three fundamental advantages. First, Excel benefits from near-universal user familiarity. Most business users already understand basic interactions and know enough to accomplish common tasks without specialized training. Second, tables maximize the amount of visible data on screen. Displaying multiple records simultaneously, with columns clearly delineated and rows sortable, allows users to perform comparisons and spot patterns that would be obscured by alternative layouts. Third, Excel requires no specialized software to operate; it arrives pre-installed on most business computers and runs on any reasonably modern device.

Excel's has several weakness, one of which is the absence of enforced column-level typing. In traditional relational databases, each column has a defined datatype with strict constraints. Excel allows arbitrary data in any cell and applies silent type coercion based on its interpretation of what the data should be. This flexibility can feel liberating when solving ad-hoc business problems, but it creates a hidden risk: data silently converts in dangerous ways, and those conversions are often irreversible.

Genomics research provides a clear cautionary example. Beginning in 2004, researchers discovered that approximately one-fifth of published genomics papers contained erroneous gene name conversions in their supplementary data files. Gene symbols such as SEPT2 (*Septin 2*) and MARCH1 (*Membrane-Associated Ring Finger E3 Ubiquitin Protein Ligase*) were automatically converted to dates—'2-Sep' and '1-Mar' respectively. These conversions were silent, unannounced, and permanent. A 2021 follow-up study found the problem had actually worsened, with 30.9 percent of papers now containing gene name errors. The problem became so widespread that the Human Gene Nomenclature Committee, the official body responsible for naming human genes, renamed 27 genes to avoid Excel's automatic date conversion. The corrupted data propagated downstream, corrupting pathway analysis and introducing errors into subsequent research built on those datasets. 

This demonstrates why business applications should strongly prefer typed relational data rather than un-typed data or allowing silent type coercion. All columns and fields in Stonecrop are typed so they can be mapped appropriately to a relational database; custom types can be used in both contexts. 

Tables naturally align with how users scan data-dense interfaces. In contrast, tabbed interfaces hide content behind labels, forcing users to remember what content exists in each tab to make efficient decisions. This creates cognitive overhead that makes tabs poorly suited to data-heavy business contexts. When users need to compare or reference information across multiple sections, tabbed navigation forces repeated interaction and taxes short-term memory beyond what a single-page layout would require.

Tables should be the default layout strategy for any dataset that is intrinsically relational or benefits from row-by-row comparison. When visualization genuinely communicates something that tabular data obscures, temporal sequences, hierarchical relationships, spatial organization, then alternative layouts are justified.


## 3. Gray-on-Gray as Accessibility and Comfort

High-contrast combinations such as pure black text on white backgrounds or white text on pure black backgrounds cause eyestrain during prolonged viewing. When a user views pure black text on white, the extreme contrast forces the eye's pupils to constrict sharply. The opposite, white text on black, causes pupils to dilate excessively. Both conditions create visual fatigue. Additionally, pure black backgrounds eliminate all light emission from the screen, causing pupils to dilate further and remaining dilated as the user strains to perceive the bright text. This phenomenon, called halation, causes white letters to blur into the black background, an effect particularly pronounced for users with astigmatism.

Research consistently shows that dark gray backgrounds paired with mid-tone or slightly lighter gray text reduce visual fatigue compared to extreme contrast. The reduction in visual stress persists across extended viewing sessions. This principle extends beyond personal comfort; it has cognitive implications. Sustained exposure to high contrast increases cognitive load even for users without diagnosed eye conditions.

Industrial operator interfaces formalized this approach as a design standard. The ISA-101 standard, published in 2015 by the International Society of Automation, established design principles for Human-Machine Interfaces (HMI) in process control. These interfaces are specifically designed for operators viewing screens eight or more hours daily in demanding, high-stakes environments. The standard mandates grayscale as the default visual treatment, with color reserved strictly for indicating abnormal conditions or alerts. This approach emerged from decades of operational experience and was validated through rigorous testing by the Electric Power Research Institute. Studies demonstrated that operators viewing gray screens quickly identify abnormal conditions because color carries semantic meaning—every use of color demands attention. The approach measurably improved operator response times and reduced critical errors.

The grayscale-as-default strategy accomplishes multiple objectives simultaneously. It reduces visual stress for extended working sessions. It eliminates alert fatigue by preventing every element from feeling urgent. It frees color to carry semantic weight, allowing colors to communicate distinct states: error, warning, success, information. Importantly, grayscale palettes are print-friendly and remain accessible when converted to true grayscale. While grayscale is the most accessible option for users with complete colorblindness (monochromancy), gray-on-gray can fail for other types of color blindness if differentiation relies solely on color. Complementary visual signals become mandatory: indicators should combine color with icons or texture patterns so that meaning persists even for users who perceive no color differentiation and draw extra attention for others.

The principle for implementation is straightforward: gray-on-gray should be the visual language default for all primary application surfaces and workflows. Accent colors should be used sparingly and intentionally, primarily for alerts and validation states that require immediate attention. When color is employed to convey meaning, it must always have accessible fallbacks. A red error message requires an accompanying icon, explicit text label, and ideally a visual weight shift; the user should never need to rely on color alone to understand the interface state.

## 4. Typography and Border Treatment

A business interface feels professional when it prioritizes clarity and stability over decoration. This impression emerges from two complementary choices: minimal ornamentation through low or no border-radius on UI elements, and deliberate, neutral typography using sans-serif fonts. Clean letterforms with even character spacing, combined with rectangular button corners and minimal rounding on cards, communicate seriousness and focus on content. The inverse, high border-radius, rounded buttons, script fonts, and playful typefaces, risks feeling casual or lifestyle-oriented, undermining the professional authority that business applications require.

Sans-serif fonts have become the standard for professional design because they are consistently associated with modernity and clarity. Humanist sans-serif typefaces, which retain subtle variations in stroke weight, generally offer better legibility than geometric sans-serifs. These principles should apply to component library defaults, primary workflows, and any interface element where user trust matters—settings panels, financial reports, compliance data. Multiple weights of a single font family establish visual hierarchy without introducing unnecessary variety.
